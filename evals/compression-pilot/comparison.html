Reactive Diffusion Policy / abstract

Primary paper

Initial draft 100 words

Contact-rich manipulation needs corrections that open-loop action chunks cannot make from newly arriving tactile signals. Reactive Diffusion Policy combines a slow latent diffusion planner with a fast recurrent decoder that updates actions from tactile or force feedback. An asymmetric tokenizer learns action chunks with tactile input supplied only to its decoder. TactAR provides augmented-reality contact feedback for collecting demonstrations. Experiments cover peeling, wiping, and bimanual lifting, using 24 Hz feedback. On peeling after contact disturbances, GelSight RDP scores 0.80 versus 0.15 for tactile-input Diffusion Policy. Relative trajectories and latency matching support execution; the tested feedback rate is sensor-limited.

Previous guidance 142 words

Open-loop action chunks preserve temporal structure but cannot respond to new contact signals during execution. Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP) retains chunk-level sequence modeling while separating planning from feedback-conditioned action decoding. An action-only encoder compresses demonstration chunks, and a recurrent decoder learns to reconstruct actions from latent chunks and tactile or force observations. A diffusion planner predicts these latent chunks; during execution, the decoder incorporates the latest contact observation at each action update. TactAR supplies augmented-reality contact feedback during demonstration collection. Evaluations cover peeling, wiping, and bimanual lifting. Under post-contact disturbances, GelSight RDP achieves a demonstration-normalized peeling score of 0.80 versus 0.15 for tactile-input Diffusion Policy, supporting a control-architecture benefit beyond adding touch. The planner runs at 12 Hz and the decoder at 24 Hz; the tested feedback rate is constrained by GelSight's 25 Hz stream, and higher-rate operation remains untested.

Primary paper

Initial draft 103 words

Learning real-robot manipulation from limited demonstrations can benefit from simulation without exact replication of the real scene. This study co-trains Diffusion Policy on real trajectories and either task-aware digital cousins, prior simulation data, or both. Digital cousins preserve robot, action space, task goals, and object and fixture categories; generated trajectories broaden training coverage. Across six tasks on Panda and GR-1 robots, the reported average success rate rises from 45.3% with real data to 83.2% with both simulation sources. Experiments also test unseen objects and positions. Transfer quality depends on simulation sampling ratios and approximate camera alignment, without requiring identical camera models.

CordViP / abstract

Primary paper

Initial draft 90 words

Hand occlusion makes camera-derived point clouds incomplete during dexterous manipulation. CordViP constructs object geometry from a generated digital twin tracked by FoundationPose and hand geometry from robot kinematics. Separate encoders and cross-attention learn hand-object contact and hand-arm coordination from play data before downstream diffusion-policy training. With 50 demonstrations per task, the method achieves 90% average success across four base tasks, compared with 74% for pose-informed G3Flow. Removing both pretraining objectives reduces the average to 79%. The representation supports partial occlusion, but accurate object models and continued pose tracking remain necessary.

Previous guidance 144 words

Hand occlusion can erase object geometry from single-view RGB-D point clouds. CordViP samples an image-generated object model, transforms its point cloud using FoundationPose estimates, and obtains hand geometry through forward kinematics. Geometry beyond visible depth points is thus available under partial occlusion, provided the object model is accurate and pose tracking continues. Play-data pretraining predicts geometrically derived contact maps and reconstructs arm actions from hand state and hand actions from arm state, with point clouds conditioning both reconstructions. A diffusion policy then learns action sequences while fine-tuning the pretrained encoder. With 50 expert demonstrations per task in addition to pretraining, CordViP achieves 90% average success across four base tasks versus 74% for pose-informed G3Flow. Removing both pretraining objectives while retaining the representation yields 79%, supporting a benefit beyond cleaner point clouds. Complete occlusion can still break tracking, and deformable objects challenge the rigid-pose representation.

Safe Beyond the Horizon / abstract

Primary paper

Initial draft 102 words

Sampling-based MPC can satisfy predicted constraints yet enter states that become unsafe after its horizon. Neural Shield VIMPC combines a learned discrete-time policy barrier with resampling-based rollouts. The barrier approximates a policy's worst future constraint value; rollout resampling replaces unsafe prefixes with safe ones. In drone simulations using 500 sampled trajectories, NS-MPPI avoided crashes even at a ten-step horizon. On AutoRally, a 12 m/s target, 15-step horizon and 30 trajectories yielded a 0.04 crash rate at 73.64 Hz on a CPU, compared with 0.46 for S-MPPI. These results concern an unverified barrier approximation, not a general safety guarantee.

Previous guidance 134 words

Short-horizon sampling-based MPC can miss later hazards and waste samples whose violation penalties drive their weights toward zero. Neural Shield VIMPC addresses these failures separately: a learned approximation of a policy's worst future constraint value brings post-horizon information into a discrete-time barrier descent constraint, while rollout resampling replaces unsafe states and control prefixes with safe counterparts when available. With 500 sampled trajectories, NS-MPPI alone among tested controllers had zero Drone crashes across evaluated horizons, including ten steps. At a 12 m/s AutoRally target speed, 15-step horizon and 30 trajectories per optimization iteration, its crash rate was 0.04 versus S-MPPI's 0.46, with CPU control at 73.64 Hz. These empirical improvements do not establish a safety guarantee: the learned barrier is unverified, and averaging constraint-satisfying controls can violate nonconvex safety constraints.
